With all this in mind, our research question was: “Across multiple machine learning and deep learning models, what model best predicts a player’s Value Over Replacement Player?”

## Data and Methodology
We sourced two datasets: one that contained current and former NBA players’ NCAA stats and one that contained NBA players’ historic stats. The NBA player dataset contained 1922 players with 24 columns (Figure 1) and the NCAA player dataset contained 25582 rows and 52 columns  (Figure 2). Since the NBA dataset was the only one with our VORP target statistic, we could only train our model with players that existed in the NBA dataset. In order to accomplish this, we first conducted pre-processing on both datasets to lowercase and normalize the player names. Then we conducted an inner join on both datasets, returning only the intersection of both datasets. We further filtered out all the non-numerical NCAA columns because this would result in an error when we aggregated the NCAA rows by player name. This left us with a resulting dataset with 674 rows and 76 columns (Figure 3). With our merged dataset, we conducted an 80-20% test to validate the split as well as scaled the data. This is because each stat, represented by a column, in our dataset fell on very different axes and we wanted to avoid feature dominance.

For our Methodology, we utilized four different machine learning models. We trained a logistic regression, linear regression, feed forward neural network, and XGBoost model. Both the linear and logistic regression used the standard out of the box models from Keras. For our feed forward neural network model (Figure 4), we utilized a 4 layer perceptron with a Dense layer, a Dropout layer, and 2 subsequent Dense layers. We opted for a simpler model architecture because our dataset was so small and we wanted to avoid overfitting. To further account for overfitting, we also introduced the Dropout layer that deactivates 20% of the neurons each training step. We utilized Mean Squared Error as our lost function and trained our model for 250 epochs. These hyperparameters were determined on a series of hyperparameter tests. Our evaluation metrics were Mean Absolute Error and R2 score because they would find the average distance in predictions across all players and determine how much of the variance is explained by the independent variables respectively. 
We additionally trained a gradient boosting model using the XGBoost package. Gradient boosting (Figure 5) is a technique that falls in the ensemble methods family of machine learning. Essentially, these use multiple models to improve accuracy. The mechanism behind gradient boosting in particular first makes a prediction just by predicting the average across all the examples. Then our model calculates the difference between the predictions and actual values, what we call the residuals. Then we train a shallow decision tree that finds a split that optimizes the predictive nature of the residuals. We then adjust our new prediction by incorporating an average of all players in a given split multiplied by a learning rate to prevent feature dominance. Gradient boosting is especially useful in smaller datasets like ours because it can identify features that are more predictive and is robust to scale.
## Results and Findings
The logistic regression model was employed to predict whether a drafted NBA player would be a 'success' or 'not success' based on their NCAA statistics and NBA draft data. Player 'success' was defined as having a VORP score above the median, converting a continuous regression problem into a binary classification task. We found that the training loss steadily decreased over epochs, and accuracy improved from ~52% to ~82%. This higher accuracy indicates that the model successfully learned patterns in the data. The model correctly identified most “Not success” cases, with 41/45 being accurate. The model correctly identified 37/49 “Sucess” cases. There were few false positives and a larger amount of false negatives (as seen in Figure 1.). The Area Under the Curve score, which rates separability, was an 0.82, which indicates the model is reasonably effective at ranking successful players higher than unsuccessful players (as seen in Figure 2.). The model also has a high probability when selecting more successful cases and a low probability when selecting unsuccessful cases (as seen in Figure 3.). 

We developed a linear regression model using selected NCAA statistics as independent features and NBA VORP as the dependent variable. The model’s performance was evaluated on standard regression metrics. The R² score was 0.08, where only 8% of the variability of the NBA VORP was accounted for by our model. The root MSE was 5.5, indicating our linear model was off on average by 5.5 points. The linear model proved to be an ineffective model to predict VORPs of NCAA players due to the complex relationship between college and NBA success.

 We also developed an XGBoost Regression model, due to the complex relationships between college and NBA success. We believed that the XGBoost could preform better due to its ability to handle complex data sets. It was configured with a learnin rate of 0.001 and a max depth of 4 to prevent over fitting due to our smaller data set. All three models, regardless of the loss function, provided poor results, with the R^2 score being -.0051, and test MAE, ie how far off on average the predicted VORP is, was 4.9626. 

The last model we developed was a feed forward neural network, aimed to capture non linear relationships between college and NBA success. The model consists of two dense layers, 64 and 32 neurons respectively, using ReLU activation, interleaved with a dropout layer (0.2) to mitigate overfitting. The output layer is a single dense neuron with no activation, suitable for regression tasks. The model’s R^2 score was the highest of any model, at 0.1908, however that is still not a successful score. The test MAE was 4.7526, indicating a substantial prediction error.
## Discussion
As evident in our results, the test mean absolute error was comparable across all models with a plateau around 4-5. Additionally, R2 score which essentially answers, How well does my model fit the data, remained below 0.25 across all models signifying it did not do a good job explaining the variability. 
In order to explore why this was happening, we investigated model performance for both the feed forward neural network and XGBoost on MAE, MSE, and Hubert Loss functions. A quick preface, the Hubert Loss functions behave like MSE for values close to the mean and MAE for outliers past a predefined threshold, essentially using the best of both loss functions. While we did not see significant improvements across Test MAE and Test R2, we did observe that MAE and Huber Loss outperformed MSE for both models (Figure 6,7). We wanted to further investigate the mechanism behind this poor performance so we visualized the distribution of Value Over Replacement target (Figure 8). As displayed in the figure, the dataset has a strong right-skew with a long tail. Furthermore as evident in the stats in Figure 9, we possess a small sample size with the data concentrated around 0. This just demonstrates the reality of the NBA and difficulties with using purely stats based methods in predicting player success. Because a majority of the league is “average”, a select number of superstars behave as severe outliers, causing our models to overestimate. In this project, we’ve experienced the outlier gravity problem due to a property of our dataset. We experimented with using differing loss functions and model architectures to try and address it.
